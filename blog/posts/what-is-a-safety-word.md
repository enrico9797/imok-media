A safety word is a secret word, agreed in advance, that tells the people you trust you need help, without anyone around you realising what's happening. You say it, or send it, and they know to act.

Families have used them for years: a word a child can say on the phone to mean "come and get me", or a code a friend can text from a bad date. Today you can also link a safety word to your phone, so sending it alerts several people at once.

This guide explains how safety words work, when they're useful, and how to set one up so it's there if you ever need it.

:::takeaways
- A safety word is a secret word that means "I need help" to people you trust, and nothing to anyone else.
- Pick a word you'd never type by accident, but can remember under stress.
- Agree in advance what your people should do when they get it.
- A safety word is not an emergency service. If you're in danger, call 999.
- If you can't speak on a 999 call from a mobile, the police Silent Solution lets you press 55.
:::

## What a safety word is for

A safety word helps in situations where you can't say "I need help" out loud, or don't want to make a scene:

- **A date or meeting that feels wrong.** You want a friend to call with an "emergency" so you can leave.
- **Walking home late.** You're uneasy and want someone to know where you are.
- **A party or night out.** You've lost your friends and want someone to come and find you.
- **Someone is with you.** You can't speak freely, but you can type one word.
- **Teenagers.** A word your child can send to mean "pick me up, no questions asked tonight".

What it isn't: a replacement for 999. If you or someone else is in immediate danger, call the emergency services.

## How to choose a good safety word

A good safety word is:

- **Unusual.** "Help" or "yes" will go off by mistake. "Pineapple" or "blue umbrella" won't.
- **Easy to remember under stress.** Pick something with meaning to you.
- **Easy to type quickly.** One or two short words.
- **Secret.** Only you (and, if it's a shared word, the people who'd receive it) know it.

:::tip Practise once
Send your safety word once, on a calm day, with your people expecting it. It's the best way to check it works and that everyone knows what to do.
:::

## Agree what happens when it's sent

A safety word is only as useful as what happens next. Agree with your people in advance:

| Question | Example answer |
|---|---|
| What should they do first? | Call me straight away |
| What if I don't answer? | Try again, then call another person on the list |
| Should they come to me? | Yes, if they know where I am |
| When should they call 999? | If they can't reach me and think I'm in danger |
| How do I say it was a mistake? | Message them to say I'm OK |

Keep the plan simple. In the moment, nobody wants to read instructions.

## If you can't speak on a 999 call

The police have a system for exactly this. According to the [Independent Office for Police Conduct](https://www.policeconduct.gov.uk/node/4903), if you're in danger and can't speak, you can dial 999 from a mobile, listen to the operator, and respond by coughing or tapping if you can. If you're prompted by the automated Silent Solution system, press **55** to be put through to the police.

It's worth knowing that a silent call doesn't automatically bring police to your location, so responding to the prompts matters. If it's safe, speaking, even in a whisper, is the best way to reach someone.

## A safety word on your phone

You can keep a safety word just between you and a friend, by text or a call. The drawback: it only reaches one person, and only if they see it.

A safety word linked to a service can alert several people at once, share your number and, if you choose, your location, and look harmless on your screen.

### How the I'm OK safety word works

I'm OK includes a safety word, free for everyone. You pick one secret word. If you ever need help, you send it to I'm OK on WhatsApp.

1. **Your people are alerted straight away.** Everyone in your daily I'm OK circle gets a message saying you may need help, with your number to call.
2. **Your screen stays discreet.** You just see "👍", and a button to share where you are if you want to.
3. **They can say they're on it.** When one person taps "I'm on it", the others are told, so nobody is left wondering.
4. **Sent it by mistake?** Reply that you're OK within 2 minutes and I'm OK offers to tell everyone it was a false alarm. After that, message them yourself.

## Places that feel unsafe

If a particular street, park or underpass makes you feel unsafe, you can tell the police without reporting a crime. [StreetSafe](https://lancashire.police.uk/help-advice/personal-safety/street-safe), available at police.uk/streetsafe, lets you drop a pin on a map. It isn't for emergencies or reporting crimes, but it helps the police and councils see where people feel unsafe.

## Safety words for families

For children and teenagers, a family safety word is a simple way to say "get me out of here" without losing face in front of friends.

- **Agree the deal.** For many families it's "pick-up, no questions asked tonight; we'll talk tomorrow".
- **Keep it secret.** Not shared with friends, even best friends.
- **Change it if it leaks.** Pick a new one if it's ever used as a joke.

If your teenager walks home after dark, a reminder at the time they usually get home ("Text when you're home 🏠") is a gentler everyday check, saving the safety word for when it's really needed.
